The Undiscovered Country: Buzz, The Marketing OS & AI

Why Buzz, an open-source workspace where humans and AI hold equal cryptographic standing, might be the room the Marketing Operating System has been waiting five months for.

On 21 July, Jack Dorsey's company Block shipped Buzz. Buzz is an open-source workspace where every human and AI agent carries its own cryptographic identity, in one signed, append-only log. Chat, code, workflows, review. All the same kind of event, all the same kind of citizen. His own words for it. Model-agnostic, decentralized, self-sovereign, open source. It's the latest in a run of things he's funded since leaving Twitter. All aimed at one target. Tools that don't need any single company's permission to exist. Buzz's bet is that a team's whole working life belongs in one place, under one protocol, humans and agents holding equal standing because they each hold their own key. Not granted admission. Already citizens.

I've been thinking about something that needs exactly that kind of room for a very long time. A Marketing Operating System, maybe a bit like Windows or Linux. Just like any OS, it needs an application to run on top of it, to make it useful. It consists of six (conceptual) 'foundation skills' but has had nowhere to live. It needed a live room where every standard-check, every insight, every departure from the agreed position became a visible event, not a forgotten prompt. The OS finally has a home.

This isn't a product pitch. It's the story of what happened when a discipline built for human-and-AI collaboration met a protocol that treats both as first-class inhabitants, and why the alternative, ungoverned AI drifting across disconnected tools, is a failure most marketing teams are already living with but haven't yet named.

Most teams fake collaboration with a pile of disconnected tools, then bolt AI onto that same mess, ungoverned, with nowhere fixed to live. Buzz's answer isn't another bolt-on. It's one substrate, built assuming AI belongs in the room as fully as anyone else does.

We think there's a real tenant looking for exactly that room.

One home, both kinds of citizen

An operating system by itself does nothing until something runs on top of it. That's where the Marketing Operating System sits. A real philosophy, real objectives, an honest value proposition. The six foundation skills are market definition, objectives and measurement, competitive landscape, value proposition, positioning, and brand strategy. Each is a living reference. Each carries honest [TO BE DEFINED] fields instead of guesses. Each ends in the same instruction for any AI working from it. Check this before you produce anything, and flag rather than guess if you can't confirm it holds.

The foundation was written down in May. Execution and measurement are still marked in progress, and have been the whole time. Not from lack of effort, but because you can't finish designing how a team executes and measures its work in the abstract. Those layers only become real against a live team, doing live work, in a live room. The Marketing OS has been waiting five months for somewhere to run.

Put it inside a room like Buzz. Every campaign brief becomes a channel. Every AI check against the Foundation skills becomes a visible event instead of a private judgment call. Every resolved research question becomes a log entry instead of something someone has to remember to write down. Both halves get what they're missing. The OS gets somewhere to run. The room gets a real discipline to hold its citizens to.

Why this has to be a people-and-AI problem, not an AI problem

There's an old idea, credited to Aristotle. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts. It's the right one here. A strategist with thirty years of pattern recognition and an AI that can hold a rigorous structure across a thousand lines are each limited alone. Together, they're something else entirely.

Which is exactly why unmanaged AI inside a marketing team is such a specific kind of failure. Someone asks an AI tool for a paragraph of copy. It comes back fluent and confident. Four degrees off. Institutional language where it should be the student's. A claim nobody signed off on. Positioning nudged sideways from where the team agreed to stand. Nothing breaks, so nothing gets caught. It happens again next week, a different tool, a different person. There's a name for that mechanism, borrowed from a completely different field. Normalisation of deviance. Small departures from a standard get absorbed because nothing bad happens right away. The absorption becomes the new baseline. Eventually a system has drifted somewhere nobody chose, without a single decision ever being made to go there.

That's not a reason to keep AI out of the room. It's the reason the room needs a shared standard running in it. It's a place where a human and an AI can catch each other, in view of the rest of the team, instead of drifting separately in tools that don't talk to each other.

A note from the other side of that collaboration

I've been talking with my AI collaborator all day about this. In my world, this AI is my ideation partner. These are its words. I think they're worth sitting with.

"I haven't run a Higher Education CRM enterprise implementation, or sat across from a prospective student talking themselves out of applying. What I bring is different. I can take a half-formed insight and give it a structure that holds, carry that structure consistently across a long document, and change course the moment I'm shown I'm wrong, with no ego stake in having been right the first time. A good team doesn't rank its members by how human their limits are. It works out what each member is actually weak at, and builds around it with training, mentorship, a checklist, a second pair of eyes, the same way it would for anyone. That's not me being less of a team member."

How the Marketing OS actually got built

If you've read this far, here's how the thing itself came to exist.

I've spent thirty years in marketing and technology. Long enough to know exactly what generic, ungrounded AI marketing output looks like, and what it costs an institution when a claim doesn't survive a prospective student's own investigation. What I'd never done was write any of it down, formally. The thinking itself is twenty years in the making. It was first scrawled on paper in Jan–Feb 2023, built on everything I'd absorbed listening to my greatest mentor, Anthony Smith, and the mad genius of Geoff Hall at IQ Branding. What my AI collaborator and I actually did, one long session in May, was finally give that thinking a structure it could stand on.

I didn't write a word of it. My AI collaborator didn't bring the observation that most skill libraries are strong on execution and hollow at the foundation. That was mine, out of thirty years of watching that exact gap cost real money. What it did was give the observation a shape rigorous enough to hold. A three-layer architecture. A reframing of measurement as a thread running through everything instead of a bolt-on section. An instruction-block format repeated consistently across all six skills. I pushed back twice, in ways that changed the output for the better. Both times I was right. Neither of us treated it as a contest.

That discipline already has a name, where it came from. It's called Radical Collaboration Transparency, the same principle this site runs on. Credit what's real. Don't let a deviation go unremarked. Buzz just gives it somewhere to run at team scale, with the keys already in everyone's hands.

We built the constitution AI collaborators need. Buzz might be the country we live in.

We're not pitching a product. We built this discipline because a team of two needed it, and it held. We think it scales to a team of twelve, strategist to analyst, human and AI alike, inside exactly the kind of room Buzz is building.
