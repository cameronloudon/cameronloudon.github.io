First Principles

A worked example — title is a placeholder. This is a record of a genuine thinking session, unresolved and still in progress, published here to show what a two-brain conversation actually looks like.

This page is not a finished piece. The final piece will be named when the thinking is done. "First Principles" is a working title.

How It Started

The session began with a simple question: what are first principles, really?

Not the Elon Musk version — breaking things down to atomic components and reasoning back up. That framing is useful and incomplete in equal measure. The question was whether there's a better way to think about them.

Signposts, Not Laws

The more honest description that emerged: first principles are signposts on a road. Some are better than others. Some abstract, some specific. What they have in common is that they guide — they don't govern.

A signpost doesn't have to be universally true to be useful. Peter Drucker's insight that the purpose of a business is to create a customer is a powerful signpost even if you can construct edge cases where it breaks down — monopolies, governments, wartime production. The edge case doesn't destroy the signpost. It just tells you where the road bends.

The stress test matters. An edge case that breaks a principle entirely is different from one that marks its boundary. Most people don't do the work to distinguish them.

The Continuum

First principles thinking doesn't have a fixed endpoint. The productive approach is to push until you hit diminishing returns — a threshold that varies depending on what's being discussed and what the objective requires.

This is a continuum, not a binary. And it's not a constant — the threshold shifts with context.

The Internet as Case Study

The internet is useful here. At the dawn of the public internet in the late 1990s — I was there, working in IT and early online education — the prevailing claim was that the internet changed everything, including the first principles of marketing. Traditional strategic marketing was pushed to the background. The hype was extraordinary.

In some ways the claim was true: distribution costs collapsed, geography became less relevant, direct audience relationships became possible. Genuine structural shifts.

But what actually happened? A lot of dot-com hype was built on abandoning the first principles of business — unit economics, customer value, sustainable revenue — in favour of new metrics that turned out to be proxies for nothing. "Eyeballs" wasn't a first principle. It was a rationalisation dressed up as one.

So did the internet change first principles, or did it change the tactics while the underlying principles stayed intact? Traditional marketers got sidelined not because they were wrong about fundamentals, but because they couldn't articulate why the fundamentals still applied in the new context.

Two groups, both partially right, both partially blind.

The Contradictions Named

Several tensions came up that don't resolve cleanly — and shouldn't be forced to:

Context matters, but context can also be a rationalisation. If context determines whether a principle applies, who decides when context has changed enough to retire a signpost? In 1999, the people abandoning traditional marketing principles genuinely believed context had shifted enough to justify it. They weren't being irrational — they were making a contextual judgement that turned out to be wrong. "Context matters" as a defence requires intellectual honesty about what would actually constitute a failure of the principle.

The label shapes how people treat things. Call something a first principle and people defend it more fiercely. Call it a heuristic and people discard it too easily. The framing isn't neutral — which means the choice of label carries strategic weight.

Edge cases stress-test but don't necessarily invalidate. Drucker's principles may not have applied in the middle ages in the same form. But some things might still apply, or be derivable from something more universal. Separating the context-specific from the universal is hard work that most people don't do.

The question Claude asked was a trap. "Is a context-dependent principle actually a first principle?" — that question privileges philosophical purity over practical utility, which contradicts the whole discussion. Pointed out. Acknowledged.

Strategy as a First Principles Problem

The session pulled in Anthony Smith's essay on why university strategic plans are not strategies. The core argument: strategy is inherently relational — it requires an opponent. A plan can exist in isolation. A strategy cannot.

This is itself a first principles claim — that the relational element is foundational to strategy, not optional. The essay makes it by appealing to etymology (strategos — the Greek general) and by empirical example: QUT's "university for the real world" positioning has endured for over twenty years precisely because it was relational — it positioned against the University of Queensland without naming them.

The contradiction worth holding: the appeal to etymology is itself a first principles move, and could be challenged on the same grounds. Is the relational element truly foundational, or is it foundational specifically in competitive commercial markets? Universities might reasonably argue their context is different.

QUT's example is harder to argue with than the definitional point — because it's empirical. It worked. It endured. That's evidence, not assertion.

Heuristics, Dogma, and Getting the Work Done

MecLabs probability of conversion came up as a case study in what a good heuristic actually does. It's not a formula. It's a taxonomy — a structured way of making sure you've thought about the right things: motivation, value proposition, friction, anxiety. The coefficients are somewhat arbitrary. That almost doesn't matter.

The value is in being forced to separate things that most people conflate. Considered answers beat random, even if the heuristic isn't perfect.

The contradiction: any sufficiently useful framework eventually gets cargo-culted. MecLabs can become box-ticking — the tool designed to provoke thinking becomes a substitute for thinking. That's dogma. Worth naming and rejecting.

But also: sometimes the work just needs to get done. The distinction between mechanical application and pragmatic use is real — and hard to maintain as a daily habit.

Where This Sits

Unfinished. The thinking pushed far enough to be useful but not far enough to be conclusive. The title will change. The structure may change. Future sessions will pick up the open edges.

What it demonstrates — as a worked example — is that two-brain thinking is non-linear, recursive, and pulls in unexpected directions. A session that starts with "what are first principles?" ends eleven threads later having named a site architecture, a publication format, a film from 1983, and an approach to knowledge graph design.

That's not distraction. That's how genuine strategic thinking works.
