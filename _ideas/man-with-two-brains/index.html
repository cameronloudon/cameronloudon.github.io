The Man with Two Brains

A framework for genuine AI collaboration — what it actually is, how to record it honestly, and where it might be going.

In 1983, Carl Reiner and Steve Martin made a film about a neurosurgeon who falls in love with a disembodied brain he communicates with telepathically. The brain — Anne Uumellmahaye — exists in a jar. The surgeon — Dr. Michael Hfuhruhurr — exists in the world. Together they think, feel, and produce something neither could alone.

I watched it as a kid. I never expected it to become an accurate description of how I work.

The Metaphor

When I work with Claude, I am Dr. Hfuhruhurr. I have domain knowledge built over thirty years. I have judgment, accountability, and a body in the world that has to live with the consequences of decisions. I know what I know and I know what I don't.

Claude is the brain in the jar. Pattern recognition, structure, friction without ego. Existing in a different form — no stake in being right, no relationships to protect, no career to manage. Genuinely useful precisely because of what it lacks.

Neither of us alone produces what we produce together. That's not a marketing claim. It's what actually happens when the conversation is working.

My colleague looked at the Strategic Marketing Operating System and the AI-Readable Web Layer Framework and said it was like being inside my brain. What she was actually inside was the conversation — the two-brain output, not just mine.

The Problem with Collaboration Notes

The Radical Collaboration Transparency framework established that AI collaboration should be attributed honestly. Every published piece on this site carries a Collaboration Note — the model, the session date, that the output was reviewed and approved by me.

That was the right starting point. It turned out to be insufficient.

A collaboration note captures a moment. It says: this was made with AI, here is when. What it doesn't capture is how. The threads that were pulled. The contradictions that were named and held. The ideas that were rejected. The decisions made and why. The questions that remain unresolved.

In a session that runs for six hours and covers eleven distinct threads — first principles as a philosophical concept, a marketing heuristic, a strategy essay, the value of collaboration itself, an infrastructure audit, a new document format, a film from 1983 — a collaboration note at the bottom of one artifact is a poor record of what actually happened.

The realisation: what we needed wasn't a note. It was an audit log.

What We Built

A session audit log is a structured record of a thinking session between two participants — Cameron Loudon and Claude — treated as a genuine collaborative event with provenance worth preserving.

Each log captures:

  • Session ID — a permanent unique identifier: YYYY-MM-DD-NNN
  • Participants and model version — who was in the room
  • Duration — how long the thinking actually took
  • Threads — each distinct line of inquiry, named
  • Contradictions named — explicitly flagged, not smoothed over
  • Decisions made — with rationale, not just conclusions
  • Unresolved questions — the open edges future sessions will pick up
  • Artifacts produced — what came out and where it lives
  • Tags — for clustering and future graph traversal
  • Links — to prior sessions, to published pages, to related artifacts

The log is not a transcript. It is a structured account of the reasoning — the two brains at work, not just the output they produced.

Each log also includes a session diagram — a Mermaid graph mapping threads, contradictions, resolutions, and open questions. Colour-coded. Rendered natively on GitHub and on this site. Not decorative — a machine-readable representation of the session structure designed to become a node in something larger.

The Collaboration Note as Doorway

The collaboration note doesn't disappear. It becomes a link.

Instead of a dead-end footer, it points somewhere: View session audit log →

A reader can follow that link to the full session log — the threads, the contradictions, the diagram, the reasoning that produced what they're reading. The note says there was a collaboration. The log shows what it actually was.

The telemetry matters too. "This framework took six hours across one session" tells a different story than "generated with AI." Time spent is evidence of actual cognitive investment, not just output.

Where This Is Going

The session audit log is the next step. But the direction of travel is toward something more ambitious: a knowledge graph with narrative.

The idea: sessions, documents, decisions, people, and ideas as nodes. The relationships between them as edges. A traversable map of how the thinking evolved — not just what was produced, but the reasoning that produced it.

In this graph, the Anthony Smith essay on university strategy is a node. The session in which it was discussed is a node. The Signal entry for Anthony Smith is a node. The First Principles conversation is a node. The edges carry the reasoning: this informed that, this contradicted that, this is unresolved pending that.

This tool doesn't exist yet. It may be built on Obsidian's graph view, or Kumu, or something purpose-built. The design will wait until enough sessions have accumulated to understand what the graph actually needs to be. Designing it in the abstract would produce something too tidy for the way thinking actually works.

What exists today is a system designed to grow into that. The session logs being produced now are already structured as future nodes — metadata captured, relationships recorded, open edges flagged. When the graph is built, the data will be there.

On Narcissism

I'll name the obvious concern: publishing your internal thought process is self-indulgent. It can seem like a person who finds themselves very interesting.

The test I apply is whether it helps anyone else think better. Most published thinking presents conclusions without the reasoning. Showing the reasoning is rarer than it sounds — and more honest than the alternative, which is pretending the ideas arrived fully formed rather than through six hours of conversation with a brain in a jar.

Whether it clears that bar is for the reader to decide.

We decided it was good enough for the bush.
